# In AI We Trust? Effects of Agency Locus and Transparency on Uncertainty Reduction in Human–AI Interaction

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Liu 2021_In AI we trust_.pdf)

**Summary**: A 2 × 3 × 2 between-subjects online experiment (N = 491) demonstrates that machine-learning AI (with machine-generated rules) reduces social presence and increases uncertainty compared to human-programmed AI, and that real—but not placebic—transparency reduces uncertainty and restores trust, with the mechanisms differing between the two AI types.

**Sources**: Liu 2021_In AI we trust_.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Liu (2021) examines how two features of a decision-making AI system—agency locus (human-made rules vs. machine-learned rules) and transparency (no explanation / placebic explanation / real explanation)—affect users' uncertainty, trust, and use intention. Two tasks were used to enhance external validity: fake news detection and personality assessment (agreeableness). The concept of "agency locus" is introduced as the degree to which the rules governing an AI system's decisions originate from humans (human-agency AI) or from machine learning (machine-agency AI). Placebic transparency—explanations that have the form but not the content of a real explanation (analogous to Langer et al.'s 1978 copy-machine study)—was included to test whether users process AI explanations carefully or treat them as mere heuristic signals.

The study was conducted as an online experiment via a Qualtrics panel with quota sampling to match U.S. Census demographics (N = 491, mean age 46.8).

Domain: general public / AI decision-making (fake news detection, personality assessment).

## Key Findings

- Machine-agency AI induced significantly lower social presence than human-agency AI (B = −0.33, p = 0.009, Cohen's d = −0.20), supporting the agency locus hypothesis. [domain: general AI/media]
- Lower social presence mediated the effect of machine-agency AI on greater uncertainty and lower trust in judgements, trust in system, and use intention (all indirect effects significant). [domain: same]
- Real transparency significantly reduced uncertainty (B = −0.90, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.64) and enhanced trust (in judgements, system, and use intention) via uncertainty reduction. [domain: same]
- Real transparency also attenuated the indirect negative effects of machine-agency AI on uncertainty and trust, suggesting transparency can compensate for the reduced human-likeness of ML systems. [domain: same]
- Placebic transparency had no significant effect on uncertainty or trust for machine-agency AI; it reduced uncertainty and increased trust only for human-agency AI (interaction significant for trust in judgements: ΔR² = 0.01, F = 9.30, p = 0.002). [domain: same]
- Placebic transparency did increase use intention across both AI types (B = 0.42, p = 0.015), suggesting that even the form of an explanation (without informational content) influences behavioural intentions through heuristic processing.
- Users of machine-agency AI were more motivated to scrutinise explanations carefully; human-agency AI users accepted lower-quality explanations because their uncertainty was already lower.

## Transparency Constructs

The paper operationalises transparency as the provision of rationales for AI decisions. Three levels are distinguished:

- **No transparency**: no explanation provided.
- **Placebic transparency**: tautological non-informative explanation (e.g., "this news is judged fake because it is identified as not real")—tests [[process-transparency]] form without substance.
- **Real transparency**: substantive, informative explanation (e.g., "this news is judged fake because its contents contradict other news sources")—genuine [[process-transparency]] and [[outcome-transparency]].

The distinction between real and placebic transparency is the paper's central methodological innovation, testing whether users' responses to explanations are driven by information processing or heuristic cues. This operationalisation reveals that transparency functions differently for users of different AI types and under different motivational conditions.

## Trust Constructs

Trust is decomposed into three distinct constructs:

1. **Trust in judgements**: confidence in the correctness of specific AI outputs (9-item adapted Lee & See, 2004 scale)—closest to [[cognitive-trust]] in AI predictions.
2. **Trust in system**: perceived competence and credibility of the AI system (12-item McCroskey & Teven, 1999 scale)—an [[algorithmic-trust]] / institutional competence measure.
3. **Use intention**: behavioural intention to adopt the system (4-item scale)—[[behavioural-trust]].

Uncertainty is measured separately (6-item Clatterbuck, 1979 scale: ability to predict and explain the AI system's behaviour) and positioned as a mediator between agency locus/transparency and trust—grounding the model in uncertainty reduction theory (URT). Social presence mediates the agency-locus → uncertainty path.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: The paper makes an important theoretical contribution by distinguishing trust in specific judgements, trust in the system, and use intention as separable trust constructs—each may respond differently to transparency. It operationalises transparency along a quality dimension (real vs. placebic) and tests whether users actually process AI explanations informationally or treat them as reassuring signals. This is a more nuanced operationalisation than studies that simply compare explanation vs. no explanation.

**RQ2**: The paper shows two distinct mechanisms for transparency's positive effect on trust: (a) informational, via uncertainty reduction (real transparency), and (b) heuristic, via the form of explanation acting as a quality signal (placebic transparency for human-agency AI). The finding that placebic transparency increases use intention even without informational content—and that this holds for both AI types—provides evidence for a heuristic-cue pathway in transparency–trust relationships. The interaction with agency locus (transparency effects differ between human-made and machine-learned AI) demonstrates a conditional, moderated relationship rather than a universal positive effect.

**RQ3**: The paper does not manipulate or measure user expertise or AI literacy as a moderator. However, the finding that machine-agency AI users scrutinise explanations more carefully implies that user motivation (driven by uncertainty) modulates depth of processing—a finding adjacent to expertise effects, since experts may also process AI outputs with greater scrutiny. Future work should test whether AI literacy or domain expertise affects the relative weight of informational versus heuristic transparency cues.
