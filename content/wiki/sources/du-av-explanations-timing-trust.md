# Look Who's Talking Now: Implications of AV's Explanations on Driver's Trust, AV Preference, Anxiety and Mental Workload

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Du et al._Look who is talking now.pdf)

**Summary**: A within-subjects driving simulator experiment (N = 32) demonstrating that the timing of automated vehicle explanations critically determines their effect on trust and preference, with before-action explanations significantly outperforming after-action and no-explanation conditions, while simply providing any explanation is insufficient.

**Sources**: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Du, Haspiel, Zhang, Tilbury, Pradhan, Yang, and Robert (2019), published in *Transportation Research Part C*, investigated how the timing of an automated vehicle's (AV) verbal explanations influences drivers' trust, preference for the AV, anxiety, and mental workload. The study used a high-fidelity driving simulator at the University of Michigan Transportation Research Institute, simulating an SAE Level 4 AV in urban, highway, and rural environments.

The within-subjects design exposed each of 32 participants to four explanation conditions (counterbalanced via Latin square): (1) no explanation (NExpl), (2) explanation before the AV acted (BExpl, 7 seconds prior), (3) explanation after the AV acted (AExpl, within 1 second after), and (4) explanation before action with driver permission required (PermReq). Each condition contained three unexpected events (e.g., oversized vehicle ahead, road reroute, approaching police vehicle). The study is grounded in Uncertainty Reduction Theory (URT), which posits that explanations reduce uncertainty and therefore should promote trust.

## Key Findings

- Before-explanation condition (BExpl) produced significantly higher trust ratings than no-explanation, after-explanation, and permission-required conditions (F(3,93) = 4.814, p = .008; post-hoc BExpl vs. NExpl: p = .013; BExpl vs. AExpl: p = .018). (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- Averaged explanations (BExpl + AExpl combined) showed no significant difference from no-explanation on trust—providing any explanation is not sufficient; only before-action explanations promoted trust. (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- Driver preference was significantly higher for BExpl over NExpl (p = .013) and PermReq (p = .031), but no overall explanation effect on preference was found when timing was collapsed. (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- Anxiety and mental workload did not differ significantly across the four conditions, contrary to predictions. (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- The permission-required (PermReq) condition—designed to give drivers more control—did not improve trust ratings or preference, and participants rated the permission-seeking AV as significantly less intelligent than the before-explanation AV (p = .041). (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- Trust rankings (ordinal measure) showed PermReq ranked higher than NExpl and AExpl (p < .001), suggesting a divergence between trust measured as attitude vs. ranking—warranting caution in how trust is operationalised. (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- Results are consistent with prior AV studies using Level 2–3 automation that found before-explanations effective; this study extends the finding to Level 4 automation. (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)
- The after-explanation finding aligns with Körber et al. (2018) who also found no trust increase from post-hoc AV explanations. (source: Du et al._Look who is talking now.txt; domain: autonomous vehicles / transportation)

## Transparency Constructs

The study examines **process transparency** in the form of real-time verbal explanations of AV decisions, a form of [[natural-language-explanations]] delivered by the automation itself. Explanations communicated the reason for an action (e.g., "Oversized vehicle blocking roadway. Slowing down."), focusing on outcome-level justification rather than algorithmic internals. This operationalises [[outcome-transparency]] and connects to the broader concept of [[disclosure]] in automated systems. The timing manipulation (before vs. after) is conceptually linked to [[uncertainty-visualization]]—pre-action explanations reduce uncertainty before it is experienced, whereas post-action explanations address uncertainty retrospectively.

## Trust Constructs

Trust is measured using the Muir (1987) trust-in-automation scale (6 items: competence, predictability, dependability, responsibility, reliability, faith; 7-point scale; α = 0.923). This is a multidimensional [[cognitive-trust]] operationalisation. The trust ranking measure captures a more holistic, relative preference judgment. The study connects strongly to [[trust-calibration]] and [[appropriate-reliance]]: the authors note that trust aligned with before-explanation conditions may reflect more accurate understanding of AV capabilities, while freely-given trust without explanation may represent misaligned reliance. Preference for AV (8 items; α = 0.885) partially overlaps with [[affective-trust]].

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: The study operationalises transparency as the presence and timing of verbal explanations, and trust using Muir's validated automation trust scale supplemented by a ranking measure. The explicit operationalisation of transparency as information provided before vs. after an action is a temporal dimension rarely captured elsewhere, contributing to [[rq1-conceptualizations]].

**RQ2**: The core finding—that explanation timing (before vs. after) is a critical moderator of the explanation–trust relationship, such that after-action explanations are no better than no explanation—is a strong contribution to [[rq2-relationships]]. This non-linear / conditional pattern challenges simple "transparency → trust" assumptions.

**RQ3**: The study does not explicitly manipulate or examine user expertise. Sample consisted of 32 mostly young adults (M age = 26.9), and individual differences in desirability of control or personality are noted as a limitation. While not directly addressing [[rq3-user-roles]], the finding that the permission-seeking condition was seen as reflecting lower AV intelligence implies that user mental models of AI competence moderate responses to transparency interventions.
