# Longitudinal Effects of Visualizing Uncertainty of Situation Detection and Prediction of Automated Vehicles on User Perceptions

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Jansen et al._Longitudinal effects of visualizing uncertainty of situation detection.pdf)

**Summary**: A three-day longitudinal online study (N=50) examining how repeated exposure to uncertainty visualizations of automated vehicle (AV) perception functions affects user trust, perceived safety, and related perceptions over time.

**Sources**: Jansen et al._Longitudinal effects of visualizing uncertainty of situation detection.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Jansen et al. (2025) conducted a longitudinal within-subjects study in which 50 US-based participants watched real-world urban driving videos twice daily (morning and evening) over three consecutive days. Videos depicted an automated vehicle's perspective overlaid with live uncertainty visualizations: semantic segmentation for Situation Detection (vehicles in blue, vulnerable road users in red, traffic signs in yellow) and pedestrian intention icons for Situation Prediction. The visualizations were generated by state-of-the-art neural networks (InternImage mIoU=86.1%; pedestrian crossing AP=83.1%), faithfully conveying real-world model imperfections including misclassifications, false positives, and flickering prediction labels.

The study is the first to examine longitudinal effects of uncertainty visualizations for SAE Level 4/5 AVs in real-world driving scenarios, addressing a key gap in prior work that focused only on short-term exposures.

## Key Findings

- Trust increased significantly over the three-day period: day 3 ratings were significantly higher than day 1 (M=3.92 vs. M=3.43, adj. p<0.001). [Domain: automated vehicles / transportation]
- Perceived safety also improved significantly by day 3 compared to day 1 (M=1.60 vs. M=0.94, adj. p=0.002). [Domain: automated vehicles]
- Predictability scores increased significantly by day 3 (M=4.05 vs. M=3.83 on day 1, adj. p=0.040). [Domain: automated vehicles]
- Mental workload showed no significant changes across days or time of day, suggesting well-designed uncertainty visualizations do not increase cognitive burden. [Domain: automated vehicles]
- Situation awareness (SART) did not change significantly over time, despite participants reporting subjective gaps in understanding upcoming AV actions. [Domain: automated vehicles]
- Participants consistently rated evening sessions (simulating the return commute) higher than morning sessions on multiple dimensions including trust, driving style safety, and clarity of AV actions. [Domain: automated vehicles]
- Qualitative analysis (n=25 who provided open feedback) revealed five themes: (1) incorrect visualizations undermine trust; (2) visual clutter from colors and flickering; (3) users wanting more feedback on AV's intended path; (4) gradual familiarity building acceptance but new errors renewing concern; (5) recognition of AV benefits tempered by real-world complexity.
- Vehicle detection visualizations were most preferred; pedestrian intention prediction icons were most criticized due to flickering and perceived unreliability. [Domain: automated vehicles]
- The absolute magnitude of improvements, while statistically significant, was small (e.g., trust increased ~0.5 points on a 5-point scale over three days), suggesting modest but consistent gains. [Domain: automated vehicles]

## Transparency Constructs

The paper operationalizes transparency as **uncertainty visualization** — visual overlays on driving video that expose the AV's internal Situation Detection and Situation Prediction processes, including their imperfections. This is a form of [[uncertainty-visualization]] embedded within [[process-transparency]]: users can see not just what the AV detects but how reliably it detects it, at a pixel level. The visualizations are low-abstraction (raw neural network outputs, not simplified icons), which provides richer information but also more visual noise.

The paper contrasts this with higher-abstraction uncertainty displays (e.g., bars, percentages, or anthropomorphic symbols) used in prior work, arguing that low-abstraction, situated visualizations give users direct evidence of uncertainty causes rather than a summary metric.

## Trust Constructs

The study operationalizes trust using the Körber (2019) Trust in Automation questionnaire, specifically the **Trust in Automation** subscale (two 5-point Likert items) and the **Predictability/Understandability** subscale (four 5-point items). The study also measures **perceived safety** using Faas et al.'s (2020) semantic differentials, and **acceptance** using the Van Der Laan et al. (1997) usefulness/satisfaction scales.

Trust is conceptualized in line with Lee and See (2004): the attitude that the system will help achieve goals under uncertainty. The goal of the visualization design is **calibrated trust** (Muir & Moray, 1996) — aligning user confidence with the AV's actual reliability to avoid both overtrust and undertrust.

The main trust-relevant finding is that longitudinal exposure to authentic uncertainty visualizations gradually increased trust without inducing overtrust, consistent with [[trust-calibration]] goals.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: The paper operationalizes transparency as embedded uncertainty visualization (overlays showing raw neural network outputs for detection and prediction) and trust using validated automation-trust scales (Körber, 2019). Perceived safety is measured via semantic differentials. This contributes to understanding how transparency is defined and measured in the automotive domain. See [[rq1-conceptualizations]].

**RQ2**: The study finds a positive longitudinal relationship between exposure to uncertainty visualizations and trust/perceived safety, but no simple direct increase — trust builds gradually and inconsistencies in visualizations slow or temporarily reverse the growth. The time-of-day effect (evening > morning) suggests contextual factors moderate the transparency–trust relationship. No significant effects on mental workload or situation awareness were found. See [[rq2-relationships]].

**RQ3**: The study does not explicitly manipulate or compare user expertise levels. Participants were US-based general-public adults (not AV experts), so all findings apply to lay users. The paper notes that the sample skewed younger and does not examine expert/novice differences. See [[rq3-user-roles]].
