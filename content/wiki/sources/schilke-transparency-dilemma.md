# The Transparency Dilemma: How AI Disclosure Erodes Trust

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.pdf)

**Summary**: Across 13 experiments, Schilke and Reimann demonstrate that actors who disclose AI usage are trusted less than those who do not, and argue this trust erosion is mediated by reduced perceptions of legitimacy.

**Sources**: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Published in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* (2025), this article investigates whether disclosing AI usage compromises the trust that others place in the disclosing actor. Unlike most transparency research, which focuses on user trust *in* AI systems, this study examines trust *in humans* who use AI. The authors run 13 experiments with varied tasks (writing, analytics, artistic work), varied actors (supervisors, professors, analysts, investment funds), and varied disclosure framings to test a core hypothesis: AI disclosure reduces interpersonal trust.

The theoretical mechanism is grounded in micro-institutional theory. AI disclosure signals a deviation from the taken-for-granted norm that professional outputs should be the product of human expertise and judgment. This perceived illegitimacy—the sense that disclosed AI use is socially inappropriate—is what drives trust erosion.

## Key Findings

- Actors who disclose AI usage are trusted less than those who do not, across writing, analytics, and artistic tasks (domain: organizational/workplace). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)
- The negative disclosure effect holds across individual roles (supervisors, professors, analysts, creatives) and organizational actors (investment funds) (domain: organizational). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)
- Legitimacy mediates the AI disclosure–trust relationship: disclosure reduces perceived legitimacy, which in turn erodes trust (domain: organizational). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)
- Third-party exposure of *undisclosed* AI usage has an even more detrimental effect on trust than voluntary disclosure (domain: organizational). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)
- The trust penalty is attenuated—but not eliminated—among evaluators with favorable technology attitudes and perceptions of high AI accuracy (domain: organizational). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)
- Neither AI familiarity nor prior AI usage among evaluators significantly reduced the disclosure penalty in meta-analytic findings (domain: organizational). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)
- The effect is robust across different disclosure framings (general vs. specific) and regardless of whether disclosure is voluntary or mandatory (domain: organizational). (source: Schilke & Reimann_The transparency dilemma.txt)

## Transparency Constructs

This paper uses a distinctive operationalization of [[disclosure]]: *user transparency*, defined as an actor informing audiences about their own deployment of AI in work processes. This is explicitly distinguished from *AI system transparency* (transparency about how the AI itself works). The paper thus sits at the intersection of disclosure ethics and interpersonal trust rather than the conventional XAI literature.

The disclosure manipulation is simple (disclosed vs. not disclosed) and does not involve explanations of AI reasoning. The framing of disclosure is also varied (general vs. specific, voluntary vs. mandatory) to test robustness. This is an important conceptual distinction from [[algorithmic-transparency]] or [[process-transparency]] as typically studied.

## Trust Constructs

The primary trust outcome is [[cognitive-trust]]—measured via trust perceptions and behavioral intentions (e.g., hiring decisions, investment decisions). The authors also tap [[behavioural-trust]] through willingness-to-hire and willingness-to-invest measures. Legitimacy is treated as a mediating cognitive appraisal between the disclosure cue and trust judgment, rather than as a trust construct itself. The trust reduction is characterized as a *trust erosion* process, asymmetric in nature and driven more powerfully by negative legitimacy cues than by positive ones.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: Provides a novel operationalization of transparency as user-level AI disclosure (distinct from AI system transparency) and operationalizes trust as both perceptual ratings and behavioral intentions across workplace contexts. Legitimacy is introduced as a mediating mechanism, extending standard trust models.

**RQ2**: Documents a robust negative (linear) effect of transparency on trust—a direct challenge to the prevalent assumption that more transparency uniformly improves trust. The effect is conditional on technology attitudes and perceived AI accuracy, pointing to moderating boundary conditions. The comparison of voluntary disclosure vs. third-party exposure also reveals an asymmetric, non-linear pattern: the least harmful condition is disclosure, the most harmful is unwanted exposure.

**RQ3**: Meta-analytic results show no significant attenuation of the disclosure penalty among those highly familiar with or experienced in AI use, which is a notable null finding for user expertise as a moderator. However, favorable technology attitudes (as a proxy for dispositional openness) do attenuate the effect.
