# Building Trust in Automatic Video Interviews Using Various AI Interfaces: Tangibility, Immediacy, and Transparency

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf)

**Summary**: A field study with 152 real job applicants examining how different AI-based asynchronous video interview (AI-AVI) interface features — tangibility (avatar), immediacy (chatbot), and transparency (disclosure) — affect cognitive and affective trust in the hiring context.

**Sources**: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Suen and Hung (2023), published in *Computers in Human Behavior*, conducted a between-subjects field experiment with 152 actual job applicants across four experimental conditions (AI-AVI vs. non-AI-AVI; tangible vs. intangible AI-AVI; immediate vs. non-immediate AI-AVI; transparent vs. non-transparent AI-AVI). The study operationalizes trust as both cognitive and affective components, drawing on Glikson and Woolley's (2020) framework. The AI-AVI context is pre-employment screening, representing a high-stakes application domain. The study is among the first to probe applicants' trust in AI video interviews from both cognitive and affective perspectives simultaneously, using a validated six-item scale with principal component analysis.

## Key Findings

- **AI per se increases cognitive trust**: applicants in the AI-AVI condition reported significantly higher cognitive trust than those in the non-AI condition (F(1,150) = 8.221, p < .01, partial η² = 0.052), attributed to perceived objectivity of algorithmic assessment (domain: HR / personnel selection).
- **Tangibility (avatar presence) increases affective trust but not cognitive trust**: the tangible AI-AVI condition produced significantly higher affective trust than the intangible condition (F(1,128) = 4.083, p < .05, partial η² = 0.031). The avatar's visual social presence raised emotional engagement but did not add rational grounds for confidence (domain: HR / personnel selection).
- **Transparency increases cognitive trust but not affective trust**: the transparent AI-AVI condition (which disclosed what AI was used and how it evaluated interviews) produced significantly higher cognitive trust (F(1,128) = 4.457, p < .05, partial η² = 0.034). No significant affective trust effect was found (domain: HR / personnel selection).
- **Immediacy (chatbot responsiveness) had no significant effect** on either cognitive or affective trust. The chatbot used a standard script rather than genuine NLP, which may explain the null result (domain: HR / personnel selection).
- **Affective trust was not negatively affected by the AI condition overall**, countering concerns that AI in hiring inherently diminishes emotional acceptance — attributed to AI video interviews having become socially normalised (domain: HR / personnel selection).
- The transparency effect is explained through "explanation-for-trust" theory (Pieters, 2011): disclosing what AI is used and how it works gives applicants rational grounds to evaluate the system's competence, raising cognitive trust without necessarily increasing emotional warmth.

## Transparency Constructs

The study operationalizes transparency as **[[disclosure]]** — specifically, informing applicants in writing about (a) the AI being used and (b) how the AI evaluates their interview responses. This corresponds to [[process-transparency]] and [[outcome-transparency]] at the interface level. The transparency manipulation is minimal text-based disclosure, not algorithmic explanation in a technical sense. It connects to:

- **[[algorithmic-transparency]]**: applicants are told the algorithm exists and its evaluation criteria, but the internal model is not exposed.
- **[[disclosure]]**: the act of notifying users that AI is involved and how it operates.

The tangibility manipulation (avatar) relates to **social presence** cues rather than informational transparency. The immediacy manipulation (chatbot) relates to **interactivity cues**.

## Trust Constructs

- **[[cognitive-trust]]**: "rational, deliberate assessment" of the AI's expertise, competence, and reliability. Operationalized with three items (e.g., "The automatic interviewer was like a real expert in assessing my performance"; α > 0.7). Increased by AI use and transparency (source: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf).
- **[[affective-trust]]**: "emotionally based trust" grounded in feelings of security and comfort. Operationalized with three items (e.g., "I felt comfortable interacting with the automatic interviewer"; α > 0.7). Increased by tangibility (avatar) but not by transparency (source: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf).
- The **differential effects on cognitive vs. affective trust** are a central finding: transparency routes through cognitive processing, while humanizing social cues (avatar) route through affective response. This distinction supports a two-pathway model of AI trust formation.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: Provides a clear operationalization of transparency as disclosure-based interface design, and distinguishes cognitive from affective trust as separate constructs with separate antecedents. Demonstrates that AI involvement alone activates cognitive trust (perceived objectivity), while emotional trust requires social/humanizing cues. Measures trust through self-report Likert scales validated via PCA (source: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf).

**RQ2**: Reveals a **differential pattern** in which the same interface features do not uniformly affect both trust types: transparency raises cognitive but not affective trust; tangibility raises affective but not cognitive trust; immediacy affects neither. This dissociation challenges single-construct trust models and illustrates that effect directionality depends on which trust dimension is considered (domain: HR). The study also finds no negative trust effects from AI, contrary to algorithmic aversion predictions (source: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf).

**RQ3**: User expertise or role is not a primary focus; all participants are job applicants (non-expert lay users). The study does not examine how applicant AI literacy or prior experience moderates the findings, which is flagged as a limitation for future research (source: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf).
