# Recommendation Agent Adoption: How Recommendation Presentation Influences Employees' Perceptions, Behaviors, and Decision Quality

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.pdf)

**Summary**: A within-subject laboratory experiment (N = 20 retail assortment planners) demonstrating that a transparent recommendation agent requiring low cognitive effort to use improves sense of control, source credibility, satisfaction, and objective decision quality compared to non-transparent or cognitively demanding transparent alternatives.

**Sources**: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Bigras, Léger, and Sénécal (2019) report a controlled laboratory experiment investigating how the design and presentation of an AI-based recommendation agent (RA) influences assortment planners' perceptions, behavioral engagement, and decision quality in an organizational (retail) context. Published in Applied Sciences, the study addresses a gap in RA research which had been dominated by consumer-facing e-commerce contexts. The authors argue that employees face different adoption dynamics than consumers — they cannot choose whether to use an employer-mandated system, yet still require transparency to justify decisions to superiors.

The experiment used a within-subject design with 20 professional participants (average age 26, 11 men, 9 women) who made assortment decisions for two fictitious clothing retail scenarios using a prototype RA developed with Axure RP 8. Three conditions were counterbalanced across scenarios: T1 (non-transparent RA, low cognitive effort), T2 (transparent RA, low cognitive effort), and T3 (transparent RA, high cognitive effort). T2 presented explanations for each recommendation via a modal window; T3 required navigation to a separate page for explanations. Eye-tracking (Smart Eye Pro, 60 Hz) captured fixation counts and gaze duration on RA recommendations and explanations. Self-reported measures assessed source credibility, sense of control, decision quality, satisfaction, and intention to adopt the RA.

## Key Findings

- A transparent RA with low cognitive effort (T2) yielded significantly higher source credibility, perceived decision quality, and satisfaction compared to the non-transparent condition (T1); cognitive effort level (T2 vs. T3) did not further differentiate these perceptual outcomes (retail/organizational domain). (source: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt)
- T2 produced the strongest positive effect on sense of control: participants felt more in control with the easily-accessible transparent RA than with either T1 or T3, supporting the role of transparency in preserving user autonomy (retail/organizational domain). (source: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt)
- Objective decision quality (accuracy against a pre-determined optimal assortment) was significantly higher in T2 than in both T1 and T3, indicating that the cognitive-effort balance of transparent presentation materially affects performance outcomes (retail/organizational domain). (source: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt)
- Participants exposed to T3 (transparent, high cognitive effort) consulted RA recommendations more frequently — consistent with information overload leading to increased reliance on the system as a cognitive crutch rather than critical engagement (retail/organizational domain). (source: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt)
- Intention to adopt the RA was significantly higher for both transparent conditions (T2 and T3) compared to the non-transparent condition (T1), confirming that transparency per se drives adoption intentions independent of cognitive load (retail/organizational domain). (source: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt)
- Qualitative interview data revealed that 9 of 20 participants desired a customizable RA, highlighting the importance of keeping employees "in the loop" through collaboration rather than passive recommendation acceptance (retail/organizational domain). (source: Bigras et al._ Recommendation agent adoption.txt)

## Transparency Constructs

The study focuses on **RA transparency** defined as exposing the logical reasoning behind AI recommendations to users, operationalized as either partial or full algorithm disclosure. The two transparent conditions (T2 and T3) differ in accessibility of explanations rather than their content. This maps primarily to [[algorithmic-transparency]] (disclosing how the system makes decisions) and [[process-transparency]] (showing reasoning steps). The study also theorizes around [[outcome-transparency]] implicitly through discussion of how explanations affect perceived decision quality. The cognitive effort required to access and comprehend explanations is treated as a design moderator, demonstrating how the mechanism of transparency delivery shapes its effects.

## Trust Constructs

Trust is conceptualized through source credibility (trustworthiness and expertise dimensions) rather than an explicit multi-component trust scale. This aligns with [[cognitive-trust]] (rational, competence-based evaluation of the RA). The study additionally captures behavioral outcomes related to [[behavioural-trust]] (actual adoption intentions and fixation behavior as proxies for reliance). Sense of control is measured as a separate affective/attitudinal outcome — closest to [[affective-trust]] in terms of comfort and perceived safety with the recommendation. The study does not explicitly include institutional or dispositional trust measures.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: The paper operationalizes transparency as a design property of RA interfaces (presence/absence of explanations and their accessibility), and measures trust-adjacent constructs including source credibility and sense of control. Eye-tracking offers an objective behavioral operationalization of explanation engagement.

**RQ2**: The study reports a conditional positive effect: transparency improves outcomes only when cognitive effort to access explanations is kept low (T2 > T1), and high cognitive effort associated with transparency (T3) can negate the decision quality benefit even while increasing recommendation consultation frequency. This constitutes evidence of a non-linear, boundary-conditioned transparency-trust relationship.

**RQ3**: User expertise is not an explicit independent variable; all participants had professional assortment planning experience of comparable level. The authors note that the organizational context — where employees use mandated systems — differs fundamentally from consumer contexts, constituting an implicit acknowledgment of role-specific adoption dynamics. Future research across industries and expertise levels is recommended.
