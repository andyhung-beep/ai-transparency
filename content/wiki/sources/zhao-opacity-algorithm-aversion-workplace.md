# Perceived Opacity Leads to Algorithm Aversion in the Workplace

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Zhao et al._Perceived opacity leads to algorithm aversion in the workplace.pdf)

**Summary**: Four pre-registered experiments (N = 1,211) in Chinese workplace contexts demonstrate that perceived opacity — not inherent distrust of algorithms — drives algorithm aversion in HR decisions, and that providing transparency information and anthropomorphising algorithms can mitigate this aversion.

**Sources**: Zhao et al._Perceived opacity leads to algorithm aversion in the workplace.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Published in *Acta Psychologica Sinica* (Vol. 56, No. 4, 2024, pp. 497–514), this paper by Zhao Yijun, Xu Liying, Yu Feng, and Jin Wanglong (Wuhan University) investigates the psychological mechanism behind algorithm aversion in workplace HR settings. The research adopts a social-psychological perspective, framing algorithm aversion as a tripartite attitude construct (cognitive, affective, behavioural) and testing perceived transparency as a mediating mechanism.

Four experiments used a variety of HR decision scenarios — resume screening, recruitment/hiring, bonus allocation, and performance assessment — with samples drawn from Chinese platforms (Credamo, PowerCX) and totalling 1,211 participants.

- **Experiment 1** (N = 303): Single-factor, between-subjects; compared human vs. algorithmic agent; measured permissibility, liking, and willingness to utilise.
- **Experiment 2** (N = 179): Replicated Experiment 1 with added measure of perceived transparency to test mediation.
- **Experiment 3** (N = 180): Manipulated perceived transparency (high vs. low information about algorithm principles) to establish causality.
- **Experiment 4** (N = 549): Three-level (no-anthropomorphism algorithm vs. anthropomorphism algorithm vs. human) to test anthropomorphism as a moderating boundary condition.

## Key Findings

- Consistent algorithm aversion was found across all four experiments and multiple HR scenarios: participants rated algorithmic decision-makers lower than human managers on permissibility, liking, and willingness to utilise. [domain: HR/workplace, China]
- In Experiment 2, perceived transparency significantly mediated the algorithm aversion effect on permissibility (indirect effect: −0.50, 95% CI [−0.96, −0.08]) and a combined aversion outcome (indirect effect: −0.25, 95% CI [−0.51, −0.04]). Participants perceived algorithmic decision processes as less transparent/comprehensible than human decision processes. [domain: HR/workplace, China]
- In Experiment 3, providing more information and principles about the algorithm significantly improved permissibility, liking, and willingness to utilise (all t-tests p < 0.001, Cohen's d ≈ 0.54–0.62), confirming the causal role of perceived transparency. [domain: HR/workplace, China]
- In Experiment 4, algorithmic agents with anthropomorphic features (human-like name, communication style) were rated significantly higher on permissibility and liking than non-anthropomorphic algorithms (F(2, 546) = 3.15, p = 0.044), though the effect was modest (η²p = 0.011). [domain: HR/workplace, China]
- Algorithm aversion was conceptualised as having three dimensions: cognitive (permissibility judgements), affective (liking), and behavioural (willingness to utilise). These form a dual-perspective (employee and employer) model. [domain: HR/workplace, China]
- The "black box" phenomenon is identified as the primary driver: algorithm opacity makes decision processes incomprehensible, which triggers aversion independent of actual performance. [domain: HR/workplace, China]

## Transparency Constructs

The paper operationalises transparency as **perceived transparency** — the subjective sense that an algorithm's decision process is comprehensible and accessible. This is distinguished from objective transparency features. The key operationalisation contrasts:
- **Opaque/black-box condition**: algorithmic output with no explanation of how decisions are made.
- **Transparent condition**: additional information and decision principles provided.

This maps to [[algorithmic-transparency]] (transparency of the decision-making process) and [[disclosure]] (proactive provision of information about how the algorithm works). The paper explicitly links its findings to the theoretical foundation of [[explainability|Explainable AI (XAI)]], characterising XAI as the "glass box" antidote to the "black box" problem.

## Trust Constructs

- **[[algorithm-aversion]]**: The central dependent construct, operationalised as a tripartite (cognitive/affective/behavioural) attitude.
- **[[cognitive-trust]]**: Captured through permissibility ratings (rational acceptability judgements).
- **[[affective-trust]]**: Captured through liking ratings.
- **[[behavioural-trust]]**: Captured through willingness to utilise ratings.
- The paper notes a distinction from prior algorithm aversion literature: aversion is driven by perceived opacity rather than inherent distrust, implying that trust can be recovered through transparency interventions.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: Operationalises algorithm aversion as a tripartite (cognitive-affective-behavioural) construct and perceived transparency as a mediating psychological variable. Conceptual contribution: reframes algorithm aversion as a transparency problem, not an inherent anti-algorithm attitude. (source: Zhao et al._Perceived opacity leads to algorithm aversion in the workplace.txt)

**RQ2**: Establishes a causal chain from low transparency → algorithm aversion, mediated by perceived opacity (Experiments 2 and 3). Demonstrates that providing transparency information reverses aversion (positive effect of transparency on trust/acceptance). The anthropomorphism boundary condition (Experiment 4) introduces a non-transparency route to aversion mitigation. This adds nuance: the transparency–trust relationship is causal and can be enhanced by anthropomorphism. [domain: HR/workplace, China] (source: Zhao et al._Perceived opacity leads to algorithm aversion in the workplace.txt)

**RQ3**: Not a primary focus. The study does not explicitly vary user expertise or role. However, it does examine both employee and employer perspectives as a "dual-perspective" design, and notes that familiarity and experience with algorithms may moderate aversion (discussed but not systematically tested). (source: Zhao et al._Perceived opacity leads to algorithm aversion in the workplace.txt)
