# Does Transparency Matter When an AI System Meets Performance Expectations? An Experiment with an Online Dating Site

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.pdf)

**Summary**: A 3×3 between-subjects online experiment (N = 227) showing that the effectiveness of algorithmic transparency on user trust is moderated by whether an AI system's performance confirms, positively disconfirms, or negatively disconfirms users' prior expectations, with detailed transparency proving beneficial only when expectations are violated.

**Sources**: Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Sun et al. (2026), published in *Computers in Human Behavior*, address the inconsistent empirical record on algorithmic transparency and trust by proposing that **expectation confirmation/disconfirmation** is a key boundary condition. Drawing on the Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM; Bhattacherjee, 2001) and the Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM; Chaiken, 1980), the authors hypothesize that users rely on heuristic processing when an AI meets expectations (making detailed transparency unnecessary or even burdensome) but switch to systematic processing when expectations are violated (making detailed transparency genuinely informative).

The experiment uses a fictitious AI-powered online dating platform ("SmartMatch") with algorithmic matching for romantic partners. Participants (N = 227, recruited via CloudResearch/MTurk) were randomly assigned to one of nine conditions crossing: transparency level (absent vs. low — algorithm name + one sentence vs. high — named algorithm with six personality-factor criteria) and performance expectation confirmation (positive — more attractive matches than expected; simple — average number of matches; negative — fewer and less attractive matches). Dependent variables: perceived understanding, cognitive trust, and behavioral intentions to use the site.

## Key Findings

- **Transparency has a significant main effect on perceived understanding** (F(2, 217) = 6.445, p = .002, η² = 0.06): high transparency led to greater perceived understanding than both low (p-level significant) and no-transparency conditions (domain: online dating / matchmaking).
- **Perceived understanding mediates transparency's effect on cognitive trust** (H3 supported): indirect effect was significant for no vs. low transparency (b = -0.27, 95% CI [-0.49, -0.08]) but not for high vs. low transparency, with full mediation through understanding for both trust and behavioral intentions (domain: online dating).
- **Expectation disconfirmation moderates the transparency–understanding–trust pathway** (H4 supported, moderated mediation): the indices of moderated mediation were significant for both cognitive trust (b = -0.51, 95% CI [-0.92, -0.10]) and behavioral intentions (b = -0.72, 95% CI [-1.30, -0.17]) (domain: online dating).
- **When expectations were simply confirmed**, low transparency led to *higher* perceived understanding (M = 4.54) than high transparency (M = 3.74, p < .001). This counterintuitive finding suggests that when the AI works as expected, detailed transparency creates cognitive overload or noise rather than adding value (domain: online dating).
- **When expectations were positively or negatively disconfirmed**, high transparency led to greater perceived understanding than the low-transparency condition, supporting the systematic-processing pathway triggered by expectation violations (domain: online dating).
- **Performance expectation manipulation alone had no significant main effect** on cognitive trust or behavioral intentions (H1 not supported), suggesting expectation effects on trust only emerge when transparency level is considered jointly (domain: online dating).
- Findings advance the theoretical position that **optimal transparency is adaptive**: concise transparency suffices when AI works as expected; detailed transparency is valuable when performance deviates from expectations.

## Transparency Constructs

The study operationalizes **[[algorithmic-transparency]]** at three levels:

- **No transparency**: The dating site provides no information about how it recommends partners.
- **Low transparency**: Discloses the algorithm's name and a single sentence stating it "analyzes and matches individuals according to scientific principles." This maps to [[disclosure]] — minimal notification of algorithmic involvement.
- **High transparency**: Discloses the algorithm name ("Duet Total Compatibility System"), the theoretical principles (similarity and complementarity), and the six personality-factor criteria used. This corresponds to **[[process-transparency]]** — revealing the criteria and logic behind decisions — and partially to [[outcome-transparency]] through making the matching rationale accessible.

Perceived understanding is the psychological mechanism through which transparency affects trust, making it the central mediator in this model. The study frames this as an [[explainability]] question: what level and form of explanation best supports user comprehension and appropriate trust?

The moderated mediation design contributes to the [[rq1-conceptualizations]] literature by linking transparency operationalization directly to cognitive processing mode (heuristic vs. systematic), grounding the explanation–trust pathway in dual-process theory.

## Trust Constructs

- **[[cognitive-trust]]**: Operationalized through items assessing users' beliefs about the AI system's reliability, competence, and dependability. The primary trust outcome and the dependent variable for the moderated mediation analysis. Increased by transparency only under expectation disconfirmation (source: Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.txt).
- **Behavioral intentions**: Users' intentions to continue using the dating site. Treated as a proxy for **[[behavioural-trust]]** (adoption/continued use). Mediated by perceived understanding under the same conditional pattern as cognitive trust.
- **Perceived understanding** is not a trust construct per se but the cognitive mechanism linking transparency disclosure to trust formation — a key conceptual contribution to understanding how [[algorithmic-transparency]] works.

The study does not explicitly measure [[affective-trust]] or [[dispositional-trust]]. The focus on cognitive trust and behavioral intentions aligns with rational/competence-based trust frameworks.

Note: The "length equals strength" heuristic from HSM predicts that users treat detailed disclosures as more credible regardless of content, but this paper shows the heuristic is overridden when it creates cognitive burden under expectation confirmation. This represents a nuanced empirical contribution to the transparency–[[trust-calibration]] literature (source: Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.txt).

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: Provides a domain-specific (online dating/matchmaking) operationalization of [[algorithmic-transparency]] at three levels, with perceived understanding as the cognitive mediator linking transparency to trust. Grounds the transparency operationalization in dual-process information processing theory (HSM), contributing theoretical clarity to why and when explanations help users form trust (source: Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.txt).

**RQ2**: Provides strong evidence for a **conditional (non-linear) transparency–trust relationship**. Detailed transparency enhances understanding and trust under expectation disconfirmation but is counterproductive or neutral when expectations are met — a clear boundary condition. This directly challenges the assumption that "more transparency always helps" and aligns with findings in other domains (e.g., Kizilcec, 2016) that partial transparency can sometimes be optimal. The interaction between transparency level and expectation confirmation constitutes one of the clearest demonstrations of a moderated transparency–trust effect in this literature (domain: online dating; source: Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.txt).

**RQ3**: Not directly addressed. User expertise is not examined as a moderator. All participants are lay users unfamiliar with the specific AI system. The study does not vary user AI literacy or prior experience, which limits generalisation to expert or experienced users. This is a gap for future research (source: Sun et al._Does transparency matter when an AI system meets performance.txt).
