# Do Citizens Trust Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence? Experimental Evidence on the Limits of Ethical AI Measures in Government

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)

**Summary**: Two survey experiments with Belgian citizens test whether information on six types of ethical AI measures (legal compliance, ethics-by-design, data-gathering limitations, human-in-the-loop, non-discrimination, and technical robustness) improves citizens' trust in governmental AI projects; results show minimal short-term effect, with pre-existing attitudes being the dominant predictor.

**Sources**: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Kleizen, Van Dooren, Verhoest, and Tan (2023) investigate whether communicating ethical AI measures to the general public — rather than direct AI users — can shift perceptions of trustworthiness, policy support, and behavioral trust (willingness to provide personal data). Published in *Government Information Quarterly*, the study comprises two pre-registered between-subjects online survey experiments conducted with representative Belgian samples (Experiment 1: n = 1,269 from a market-research panel; Experiment 2: n = 738 from a university citizen panel).

Vignettes described three hypothetical federal AI projects (road-damage detection, tax-fraud risk profiling, and crowd-flow prediction via mobile data). Experimental interventions added information on one or more of six ethical-AI conditions. Outcomes measured were perceived trustworthiness of the AI project (ability/benevolence/integrity scale), behavioral trust (willingness to provide data), and policy support.

The authors also test pre-existing attitudinal predictors: general trust in government, general trust in AI, privacy concerns, perceived discrimination, and familiarity/professional use of AI.

## Key Findings

- Across both experiments, most experimental groups showed no significant difference from the control group in perceived trustworthiness, behavioral trust, or policy support; F-tests for overall model fit were non-significant and R² values were extremely low (domain: public sector / government). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- Equivalence testing provided evidence of true null effects for many conditions, particularly those involving legal information (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- The only conditions reaching significance were "Fairness & Robustness" in Experiment 2 (modest positive effects on policy support and behavioral trust), and these effects disappeared once attitudinal control variables were added (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- Pre-existing general trust in government and general trust in AI were both strong positive predictors of behavioral trust, confirming hypotheses H2 and H3 (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- Privacy concerns had a consistently strong negative effect on perceived trustworthiness, policy support, and behavioral trust across both experiments (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- Perceived discrimination negatively predicted perceived trustworthiness and policy support but not behavioral trust (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- Professional familiarity with AI had a positive effect on policy support and, in Experiment 1, on perceived trustworthiness, but not on behavioral trust (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- Qualitative open-answer data reinforced the quantitative findings: only ~8–10 of over 1,300 responses referenced the experimental vignettes; most invoked pre-existing privacy concerns and trust in government (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)
- The study contrasts with Grimmelikhuijsen (2023), who finds that explainability improves trust among direct users confronted with an adverse AI decision — suggesting effects may be stronger when personal stakes are higher (domain: public sector). (source: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf)

## Transparency Constructs

The six experimental interventions map onto several transparency and ethical-AI constructs:

- **Algorithmic transparency / explainability**: ethics-by-design condition included "algorithmic explainability" and use of AI for societal well-being.
- **Process transparency**: ethics-by-design, legal compliance, and human-in-the-loop conditions provide information about how AI is governed.
- **Disclosure / notification**: all conditions are essentially disclosure treatments — providing citizens with information about AI practices.
- **Non-discrimination**: a dedicated fairness and non-discrimination condition.
- **Technical robustness**: a blockchain-based data-security condition.
- **Human-in-the-loop**: a condition specifying that civil servants review AI outputs before decisions are finalised.

The paper's central finding is that these disclosure/transparency measures have negligible short-term effect on non-user citizens' trust (see [[algorithmic-transparency]], [[process-transparency]], [[disclosure]]).

## Trust Constructs

The study operationalises trust in three ways:

- **Perceived trustworthiness** (cognitive): three-item scale derived from Grimmelikhuijsen and Knies (2017), assessing ability, benevolence, and integrity of the AI project.
- **Behavioral trust**: willingness to provide personal data to the government's AI projects — a revealed-preference measure closer to genuine vulnerability acceptance.
- **Policy support**: two-item scale on expected service improvement and general support for governmental AI.

Pre-existing **institutional trust** (trust in government and trust in AI) are operationalised as independent predictors and confirm the importance of general dispositional and institutional trust levels (see [[institutional-trust]], [[cognitive-trust]], [[behavioural-trust]]).

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: The paper operationalises "trustworthiness" distinctly from "trust" (following Mayer et al. 1995 and Grimmelikhuijsen & Knies 2017), separating ability/benevolence/integrity perceptions from the willingness to be vulnerable. It also distinguishes behavioral trust from attitudinal measures, addressing a known measurement gap in the literature. The EU HLEG Trustworthy AI framework and AI4People framework are reviewed as prominent operationalisations of trustworthy AI from an institutional standpoint. See [[rq1-conceptualizations]].

**RQ2**: The core finding is a near-null relationship between ethical-AI transparency disclosures and citizen trust outcomes. Pre-existing attitudes dominate; the only marginal positive effect involves "Fairness & Robustness" information. This is an important null/conditional finding — transparency communication does not straightforwardly increase trust in non-user populations. This contrasts with positive effects found in direct-user studies. See [[rq2-relationships]].

**RQ3**: The study does not examine AI-literacy or professional expertise as moderators of explanation effects per se, but does test "professional use of AI" as a predictor. Professionals show higher policy support and (in Exp. 1) higher perceived trustworthiness, but not higher behavioral trust. The paper explicitly distinguishes non-users (general public) from direct users, identifying distance from the AI system as a key moderating factor — which maps onto [[user-expertise]] and [[domain-context]]. See [[rq3-user-roles]].
