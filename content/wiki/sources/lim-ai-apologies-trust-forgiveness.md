# How Warm- versus Competent-Toned AI Apologies Affect Trust and Forgiveness through Emotions and Perceived Sincerity

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Lim et al._How warm-versus competent-toned AI apologies affect trust and forgiveness.pdf)

**Summary**: A 3 × 2 between-subjects experiment (N = 464) finds that human-attributed corporate apologies consistently outperform AI-attributed ones on trust, forgiveness, sincerity, and negative emotions, but that warm relational tone moderates these effects by reducing negative emotions in AI-attributed conditions and enhancing sincerity in human-attributed ones.

**Sources**: Lim et al._How warm-versus competent-toned AI apologies affect trust and forgiveness.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

---

## Overview

Lim, Hong, and Schneider (2025) investigate how AI versus human authorship attribution interacts with relational tone (warmth vs. competence) to influence trust and forgiveness intentions following a corporate data-breach crisis. Drawing on machine heuristic theory and the Stereotype Content Model (Fiske et al., 2007), the study presents participants with a simulated X (formerly Twitter) apology post from a fictitious watch brand, Novera, with authorship indicated by a detection-tool-style interface showing either "100% AI" or "100% Human." The design also included a no-attribution control. A 3 (authorship: AI / Human / control) × 2 (tone: warmth / competence) factorial design was used with 464 participants recruited via Prolific Academic.

The paper employs moderated mediation, with negative emotions and perceived sincerity as mediators and trust as an intermediate mediator leading to forgiveness intention.

Domain: organisational crisis communication.

## Key Findings

- Human-attributed apologies elicited significantly higher perceived sincerity (M = 4.90 vs. 4.11), lower negative emotions (M = 2.55 vs. 3.08), higher trust (M = 4.33 vs. 3.70), and greater forgiveness intentions (M = 4.60 vs. 4.10) than AI-attributed apologies across all conditions (all p < 0.01). [domain: crisis communication / corporate PR]
- Warm-toned apologies produced lower negative emotions (M = 2.53 vs. 3.00, p = 0.006) and higher perceived sincerity (M = 4.71 vs. 4.31, p = 0.018) than competence-toned apologies overall. [domain: same]
- Warmth reduced negative emotions more strongly in AI-attributed than human-attributed conditions (interaction F(1,302) = 4.40, p = 0.037): in the AI condition, warm vs. competent tone reduced negative emotion by 0.82 points; in the human condition, the difference was only 0.12 points. [domain: same]
- Warmth enhanced perceived sincerity more strongly in human-attributed than AI-attributed conditions (interaction F(1,302) = 3.95, p = 0.048): human warmth added 0.73 in sincerity; AI warmth added only 0.07. [domain: same]
- Moderated mediation was supported: warm tone reduced the negative indirect effect of AI authorship on forgiveness intention via negative emotions and trust (β = 0.20, p < 0.05, BCa 95% CI [0.01, 0.38]). [domain: same]
- Warm-toned AI apologies, while improved relative to competence-toned AI apologies, still produced lower sincerity and trust than human-attributed apologies, indicating warmth as a partial—not complete—compensatory mechanism for AI's relational deficit.
- Under a competence tone, AI authorship significantly reduced forgiveness through the negative emotion pathway, but this effect was neutralised (rendered non-significant) under warmth tone.

## Transparency Constructs

This paper does not directly study AI explainability or process transparency. However, it is relevant to [[disclosure]]: the authorship attribution manipulation uses a visual disclosure interface (mimicking AI-detection tools) to reveal whether content was AI- or human-generated. The paper thus addresses transparency in the sense of source disclosure, and shows that the manner of disclosure (AI vs. human attribution) substantially affects trust and emotional outcomes independently of message content.

The paper implicitly addresses [[algorithmic-transparency]] in the sense that audiences become aware of the generative process behind a communication, and this awareness (rather than any technical explanation) drives responses.

## Trust Constructs

Trust is measured with an 8-item scale adapted from Lim and Jiang (2021) covering integrity, dependability, and competence sub-dimensions (M = 4.06, SD = 1.25, α = .96). This is a multi-dimensional measure capturing elements of both [[cognitive-trust]] (competence, dependability) and integrity-based trust. Forgiveness intention (5-item scale, α = .93) is used as a downstream [[behavioural-trust]] outcome. Perceived sincerity functions as a cognitive mediator and negative emotions as an affective mediator, together mapping onto the cognitive–affective dual structure of trust.

The paper explicitly theorises [[affective-trust]] through the role of negative emotions (anger, annoyance, irritation, aggravation) as a pathway connecting authorship attribution to trust and forgiveness.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: The paper operationalises trust as relational and multi-dimensional (integrity, dependability, competence) in the context of AI-mediated crisis communication. Source attribution (AI vs. human disclosure) is proposed as a transparency construct—specifically, disclosure of AI involvement in message creation—and is shown to affect all downstream trust outcomes. This represents a less commonly studied operationalisation of transparency compared to XAI approaches (feature importance, explanations), with implications for how transparency is conceptualised beyond technical explanations.

**RQ2**: The paper provides robust evidence for a negative linear effect of AI authorship on trust and forgiveness that is conditionally moderated by message tone. The transparency-related variable (authorship disclosure) is negatively associated with trust when AI is disclosed, and this relationship is weakened—but not reversed—by warmth. This conditional, non-symmetrical moderation pattern is an important finding for the literature on AI transparency's effects: disclosure alone does not guarantee trust improvement; the quality and warmth of the disclosed content matter.

**RQ3**: The study does not examine user expertise or AI literacy as a moderator. The participant sample is a general U.S. adult population (mean age 46.2). The paper notes that perceptions of AI appropriateness in emotionally sensitive tasks vary with familiarity and social norms, suggesting expertise or AI experience could moderate effects, but this is not tested.
