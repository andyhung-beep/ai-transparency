# Exploring Citizens' Stances on AI in Public Services: A Social Contract Perspective

**Source file**: [Original article](../../raw/Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.pdf)

**Summary**: Using a social contract framework and qualitative interviews with 20 Norwegian citizens exposed to an AI welfare service prototype, Schmager et al. identify high institutional trust, a balanced value proposition, and human-in-the-loop transparency as the three drivers of positive citizen stances toward public sector AI.

**Sources**: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Published in *Data & Policy* (2024), this qualitative study explores how citizens in Norway perceive AI use in public welfare services. The authors employ Social Contract Theory (SCT) as an analytical lens, structuring government–citizen relationships at three levels: macro (power and legitimacy), meso (balancing individual and collective needs), and micro (interaction and agreement processes). An interactive prototype of an AI-assisted sick-leave duration prediction tool was developed and presented to 20 participants, who were then interviewed.

The study uses Action Design Research (ADR) methodology, combining prototype development with semi-structured interviews. The AI system is designed as a decision-support tool (not fully automated), with a human caseworker retaining final decision authority.

## Key Findings

- Citizens in Norway display a generally positive stance toward AI in public services, strongly shaped by pre-existing institutional trust in government (domain: public services/welfare). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)
- 40% of participants increased their comfort rating after interacting with the prototype; approximately 40% did not change; 20% decreased (domain: public services). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)
- High government trust at the macro level led some participants to consent to AI use without reading the full information disclosure, indicating that institutional trust can substitute for direct system transparency (domain: public services). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)
- At the meso level, citizens weigh individual rights against collective benefits; concerns about AI oversimplifying individual cases and perpetuating bias were common (domain: public services). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)
- At the micro level, having a human in the loop was strongly valued; citizens wanted a human point of contact for contestation; two participants favored removing humans to reduce bias (domain: public services). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)
- Transparency into processes, data usage, and model logic was positively received, but many participants did not fully understand the provided explanations; exposure to information contributed positively to stance even without full comprehension (domain: public services). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)
- Demographic-group-based predictions raised concerns about oversimplification and loss of individual nuance (domain: public services). (source: Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.txt)

## Transparency Constructs

The paper addresses [[process-transparency]] (citizens want to understand *how* the AI is used in the workflow), [[outcome-transparency]] (what the model predicts and on what basis), and [[disclosure]] (what data are collected and for what purpose). The prototype provides a process chart, a data table, and natural language explanations of the prediction.

A key finding is that the *availability* of transparency features matters more than full citizen comprehension of them—mere presence of explanations supports positive stance. This resonates with research on [[algorithmic-transparency]] as a procedural signal of legitimacy rather than a cognitive tool for understanding.

## Trust Constructs

The paper centers on [[institutional-trust]] at the macro level: trust in the Norwegian government acts as an enabling condition that allows citizens to accept AI without detailed scrutiny. At the micro level, [[cognitive-trust]] and [[behavioural-trust]] (willingness to consent) are shaped by process features—specifically human oversight and transparent explanations. The study operationalizes trust through a comfort-with-AI rating (5-point scale) and qualitative interview responses. The trust is not measured toward the AI system itself but toward the government-citizen relationship as a whole.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**RQ1**: Demonstrates that trust in AI public services is better conceptualized through a multi-level institutional lens (macro–meso–micro) rather than solely as user-level perceptions. Operationalizes transparency at three layers: data disclosure, process documentation, and model logic explanation.

**RQ2**: Illustrates how pre-existing institutional trust can substitute for system-level transparency—citizens with high government trust may not require or engage with explanations to form positive attitudes. This is a conditional (moderated) transparency–trust relationship: transparency's benefit depends on baseline institutional trust level. Partial understanding of explanations may still suffice to improve stance.

**RQ3**: Does not directly compare user expertise groups, but highlights that lower literacy with statistics and bureaucratic language creates barriers to understanding AI explanations. The qualitative design captures the general public (non-experts) as the target population, emphasizing that explanation accessibility is a design requirement for non-expert citizens.
