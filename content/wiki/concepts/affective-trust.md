# Affective Trust

**Summary**: Affective trust is grounded in emotional bonds, feelings of security, and care rather than rational assessment of competence; it is less commonly measured than cognitive trust in AI contexts but plays an important mediating role between explanation design and behavioural outcomes.

**Sources**: Bernardo & Seva_Affective_Design_Analysis_of_Explainable.pdf; Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf; Nelekar et al._Effectiveness embodied conveersational agents for managing academic stress.pdf

## Definition

Affective trust is the emotional dimension of trust — the feeling of security, comfort, and care that a user experiences toward an AI system or its outputs. It is distinguished from:
- [[Cognitive trust]]: judgment-based assessment of competence and reliability
- [[Behavioural trust]]: trust manifested in reliance and adoption decisions

Affective trust can operate through both conscious emotional appraisal and implicit affective responses to design features (anthropomorphism, warmth, responsiveness).

## Key Findings

### Affective Design Mediates Explanation → Trust

Bernardo & Seva (2023) propose and test the XAI Trust Calibration (XAITC) model, showing that XAI design affects trust through two parallel routes:
1. **Cognitive route**: explanation quality → perceived reliability → [[cognitive-trust]] → reliance
2. **Affective route**: explanation design (humanised communication, example-based formats) → positive emotions → affective trust → reliance

The affective route is empirically validated through structural equation modelling (N = 202). AI anxiety and incidental emotions are significant moderators of the affective route — users with high AI anxiety are less receptive to affective trust building through design (domain: healthcare; Bernardo & Seva, 2023).

### Affective vs. Cognitive Trust Dissociation

Suen & Hung (2023) directly compare affective and cognitive trust responses to three interface features in AI video interviews:
- **Tangibility** (avatar presence): increases affective trust but not cognitive trust
- **Transparency** (disclosure of evaluation criteria): increases cognitive trust but not affective trust
- **Immediacy** (real-time chatbot feedback): affects neither

This clean dissociation shows that different design features target different trust dimensions, and that both types of trust may be necessary for full adoption of AI systems (domain: HR/recruitment; Suen & Hung, 2023).

### Cultural Factors in Affective Trust

Nelekar et al. (2021) find that Indian university students show much higher baseline propensity to trust (M > 4.5/5) compared to Western samples, and that affective bonds (working alliance) form quickly regardless of explanation type. This suggests cultural context shapes the baseline level of affective trust, which may moderate the impact of transparency interventions (domain: education/mental health; Nelekar et al., 2021).

### Tone and Warmth in AI Communication

Lim et al. (2025) show that warm-toned AI apologies reduce negative emotions (affective response) and partially improve trust, while competent-toned apologies do not. This demonstrates that the linguistic register of AI outputs shapes affective trust independently of content accuracy (domain: general/organizational; Lim et al., 2025).

## Measurement

Affective trust is less standardised in measurement than cognitive trust. Common approaches include:
- Emotion items (positive/negative affect scales) included alongside trust scales
- Working alliance measures (task, goal, bond dimensions — used in conversational agent contexts)
- Single-item affective responses (e.g., "I feel comfortable relying on this system")
