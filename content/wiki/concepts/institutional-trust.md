# Institutional Trust

**Summary**: Institutional trust is trust directed at organisations, governments, or regulatory systems that deploy or oversee AI; it is frequently found to dominate or substitute for system-level transparency effects, making prior institutional trust a critical boundary condition for XAI interventions.

**Sources**: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf; Schmager et al._exploring-citizens-stances-on-ai-in-public-services-a-social-contract-perspective-div.pdf; Esmaeilzadeh et al_2021_Patients_ perceptions toward human-artificial intelligence interaction in health care.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-18

## Definition

Institutional trust refers to confidence in the systems, organisations, and regulatory frameworks within which AI is embedded. In AI contexts it encompasses:
- Trust in the government or public agency deploying an AI system
- Trust in regulatory bodies overseeing AI development
- Trust in the healthcare system or hospital deploying a clinical AI
- Confidence in audit and certification processes

It is distinct from [[cognitive-trust]] (trust in the specific AI system) and [[algorithmic-trust]] (trust in algorithmic decision-making generally).

## Key Findings

### Institutional Trust Dominates Transparency Interventions

Kleizen et al. (2023) tested whether communicating seven ethical AI measures (legal compliance, explainability, human-in-the-loop, non-discrimination, technical robustness) to citizens affects their trust and policy support for governmental AI systems. Results: near-null effects from all transparency interventions. Prior trust in government (β = 0.3–0.6), general AI trust, and privacy concerns are far stronger predictors than ethical AI messaging (β = 0.05–0.15). Short-term disclosure cannot overcome weak institutional foundations (domain: government/Belgium; Kleizen et al., 2023).

### Institutional Trust as an Enabling Condition

Schmager et al. (2024) apply social contract theory to citizen attitudes toward AI in Norwegian public services. They identify three levels of enabling conditions for AI acceptance:
1. **Macro level**: high baseline institutional trust in government
2. **Meso level**: balanced value proposition (individual vs. collective benefit)
3. **Micro level**: process and data transparency, human-in-the-loop oversight

This multilevel model suggests institutional trust is a necessary (but not sufficient) enabler — without it, micro-level transparency mechanisms have limited effect (domain: government/Norway; Schmager et al., 2024).

### Healthcare Institutional Context

Esmaeilzadeh et al. (2021) show that patients trust physician-only encounters significantly more than AI-only or AI-augmented encounters, suggesting that the institutional authority of the physician profession mediates acceptance of AI in clinical settings. Communication barriers, regulatory transparency concerns, and liability remain major barriers even when AI functions within established institutional frameworks (domain: healthcare; Esmaeilzadeh et al., 2021).

## Implications

The dominance of institutional trust over system-level transparency has significant implications:
- XAI interventions alone are unlikely to build trust in AI systems deployed by organisations with low institutional credibility.
- Transparency must be embedded in broader trust-building strategies (legitimacy, accountability, stakeholder engagement) rather than treated as a technical fix.
- Regulatory labeling and audit certificates may be more effective than explanations when institutional trust is weak (see also Shulner's certification-based explanation).
