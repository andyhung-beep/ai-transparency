# Suen & Hung (2023) — Methods

**Summary**: A between-subjects field experiment with 152 real job applicants crossing four AI-based asynchronous video interview (AI-AVI) interface conditions — AI presence, tangibility (avatar), immediacy (chatbot), and transparency (disclosure) — to examine their differential effects on cognitive and affective trust.

**Sources**: Suen & Hung_Building trust in automatic video interviews.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1 / RQ2

**Last updated**: 2026-04-19

---

## Study design
experimental (between-subjects field experiment)

## Sample
- N: 152 (initial); 130 used in the main analysis comparing AI-AVI interface variations (a subset participated in the transparency, tangibility, and immediacy condition arms)
- Population: actual job applicants undergoing pre-employment screening at a hiring organisation
- Domain: HR / personnel selection (asynchronous video interviewing)
- Country / region: not reported (field study conducted at a real organisation)
- Recruitment method: convenience sample of real job applicants invited through the hiring organisation

## Transparency operationalization
- Type: disclosure — text-based disclosure of AI involvement and evaluation criteria in the interview interface
- Manipulation or measure: between-subjects manipulation; transparent condition disclosed (a) which AI was being used and (b) how the AI evaluated applicants' interview responses; non-transparent condition provided no such information
- Scale / instrument name: not applicable (transparency was a binary interface manipulation)
- Number of items: not applicable

## Trust operationalization
- Type: cognitive trust and affective trust (measured as separate constructs)
- Measure: self-report Likert-scale items; cognitive trust assessed via three items capturing rational assessment of the AI's expertise and competence (e.g., "The automatic interviewer was like a real expert in assessing my performance"); affective trust assessed via three items capturing emotional comfort and security (e.g., "I felt comfortable interacting with the automatic interviewer"); scale validated via principal component analysis
- Scale / instrument name: adapted from Glikson & Woolley (2020) two-component trust framework; 6-item scale (3 cognitive + 3 affective items)
- Number of items: 6 (3 cognitive, 3 affective)
- Behavioural vs self-report: self-report

## Moderators and covariates tested
- None explicitly tested as moderators; user expertise and AI literacy were not measured or included as covariates
- Note: all participants were lay job applicants; no expertise-based moderation examined — relevant to RQ3 as a limitation

## Statistical approach
- Primary analysis method: one-way and multi-way ANOVA comparing trust scores across conditions
- Software: not reported
- Key model fit or effect size reported: F statistics and partial η² reported (e.g., AI vs. non-AI on cognitive trust: F(1,150) = 8.221, p < .01, partial η² = 0.052; transparency on cognitive trust: F(1,128) = 4.457, p < .05, partial η² = 0.034; tangibility on affective trust: F(1,128) = 4.083, p < .05, partial η² = 0.031); Cronbach's α > 0.7 for subscales

## Author-noted limitations
- All participants were from a single hiring organisation, limiting generalisability across industries and cultural contexts
- Transparency manipulation used standard text disclosure, which may not replicate findings for more detailed algorithmic explanations
- Applicant AI literacy and prior experience with AI interviews were not measured; these may moderate trust responses
- The chatbot used a scripted response system rather than genuine NLP, which may explain the null immediacy effect and limits ecological validity
- Self-reported trust may not capture actual trust-driven behaviours such as accepting or deferring to AI recommendations
