# Kleizen 2023 — Methods

**Summary**: Two pre-registered between-subjects online survey experiments with representative Belgian citizen samples (Experiment 1: n = 1,269; Experiment 2: n = 738) testing whether information about six types of ethical AI measures shifts citizens' trust in governmental AI projects.

**Sources**: Kleizen et al._Do citizens trust trustworthy artificial intelligence_.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1 / RQ2 / RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-19

---

## Study design
experimental (two between-subjects online survey experiments; pre-registered)

## Sample
- N: 1,269 (Experiment 1); 738 (Experiment 2)
- Population: general public / citizens (representative Belgian samples)
- Domain: public sector / e-government (road-damage detection, tax-fraud risk profiling, crowd-flow prediction)
- Country / region: Belgium
- Recruitment method: market-research panel (Experiment 1); university citizen panel (Experiment 2)

## Transparency operationalization
- Type: disclosure / notification (six ethical AI measure conditions)
- Manipulation or measure: between-subjects vignette manipulation — participants read descriptions of hypothetical governmental AI projects with added information on one or more of six ethical-AI conditions: (1) legal compliance, (2) ethics-by-design (includes algorithmic explainability), (3) data-gathering limitations, (4) human-in-the-loop, (5) non-discrimination/fairness, (6) technical robustness (blockchain data security); control group received no ethical-AI information
- Scale / instrument name: not applicable (vignette experiment)
- Number of items: not applicable

## Trust operationalization
- Type: cognitive (perceived trustworthiness); behavioural; institutional (pre-existing)
- Measure: (1) Perceived trustworthiness — 3-item scale assessing ability, benevolence, and integrity of the AI project (derived from Grimmelikhuijsen & Knies, 2017); (2) Behavioral trust — willingness to provide personal data to the AI project (revealed-preference measure); (3) Policy support — 2-item scale on expected service improvement and general support for governmental AI; pre-existing institutional trust in government and in AI measured as independent predictors
- Scale / instrument name: Perceived trustworthiness scale (Grimmelikhuijsen & Knies, 2017)
- Number of items: 3 (perceived trustworthiness); 2 (policy support); not reported (behavioral trust item count)
- Behavioural vs self-report: both (self-report for perceived trustworthiness and policy support; behavioural willingness measure for behavioral trust)

## Moderators and covariates tested
- General trust in government: measured; strong positive predictor of behavioral trust (H2 supported)
- General trust in AI: measured; strong positive predictor of behavioral trust (H3 supported)
- Privacy concerns: measured; strong negative predictor of all three outcomes
- Perceived discrimination: measured; negative predictor of perceived trustworthiness and policy support, not of behavioral trust
- Professional familiarity with AI: measured; positive predictor of policy support and perceived trustworthiness (Experiment 1 only); not significant for behavioral trust (relevant to RQ3 as a proxy for AI familiarity/expertise)
- Qualitative open responses: collected as supplementary check; ~8–10 of 1,300+ responses referenced experimental vignettes

## Statistical approach
- Primary analysis method: ANOVA (F-tests for overall model fit of experimental conditions); OLS regression (attitudinal predictors); equivalence testing (TOST procedure for null effects)
- Software: not reported
- Key model fit or effect size reported: F-tests non-significant for most experimental conditions; R² values extremely low across conditions; equivalence tests supported true null for legal-compliance conditions

## Author-noted limitations
- Short-term, one-shot exposure to vignettes may not capture how citizens form trust through sustained AI experience
- Sample limited to Belgian citizens; cross-national generalizability not established
- Ethical AI conditions may require greater familiarity or salience to affect non-user citizens
- Study contrasts with direct-user studies (e.g., Grimmelikhuijsen, 2023) where transparency effects are stronger; distance from the AI system identified as a key moderating factor
- Qualitative responses suggest most citizens did not process the experimental vignette information carefully
