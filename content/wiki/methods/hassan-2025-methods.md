# Hassan et al. 2025 — Methods

**Summary**: A qualitative scenario-based study using 28 semi-structured interviews and observations with clinicians from Norway and Egypt, examining how expert clinicians respond to AI opacity versus explainability in a cerebral palsy risk prediction system.

**Sources**: Hassan et al._Unlocking the black box.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-19

---

## Study design
qualitative

## Sample
- N: 28 (clinicians; approximately 17+ from Norway, remainder from Egypt)
- Population: clinical domain professionals (clinicians / neonatologists involved in cerebral palsy assessment)
- Domain: healthcare (neonatology / cerebral palsy risk assessment)
- Country / region: Norway and Egypt
- Recruitment method: not reported (professional / purposive sample of clinical practitioners)

## Transparency operationalization
- Type: explainability / process transparency — feature-importance visualizations from a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) revealing which body movement features drove the cerebral palsy risk prediction
- Manipulation or measure: within-scenario sequential presentation — clinicians first encountered the AI system in opaque form (prediction only), then with XAI insights (feature-importance visualizations of infant movement features); qualitative comparison of responses across the two states
- Scale / instrument name: not applicable (qualitative study)
- Number of items: not applicable

## Trust operationalization
- Type: cognitive trust — clinicians' belief in AI reliability and prediction validity; also appropriate reliance as a normative ideal
- Measure: qualitative — trust assessed through interview themes and observational notes; indicators include guardedness/skepticism (low trust), increased trust and learning readiness (high trust), and judgment revision after XAI disclosure
- Scale / instrument name: not applicable
- Number of items: not applicable
- Behavioural vs self-report: qualitative interview / observational (neither standard behavioural measure nor self-report Likert)

## Moderators and covariates tested
- Clinician expertise / experience level: implicitly examined; less experienced clinicians in sparse-expertise settings identified as more susceptible to learning myopia from XAI — directly relevant to RQ3
- Country / cultural context (Norway vs. Egypt): cross-national sample included but not formally compared as a moderator
- Specialty and role: radiologists vs. other specialists implicitly compared through interview themes

## Statistical approach
- Primary analysis method: thematic analysis of semi-structured interview transcripts and observational notes
- Software: not reported
- Key model fit or effect size reported: not applicable (qualitative study)

## Author-noted limitations
- Small qualitative sample limits generalisability
- Scenario-based methodology (not real clinical deployment) may not capture authentic decision-making pressures
- Cross-national sample (Norway and Egypt) introduces contextual heterogeneity that is not formally analysed
- Risk of learning myopia is identified but not empirically measured; framed as a theoretical concern
- Unconditional trust in AI explanations was not controlled — some clinicians may have simply deferred to XAI without critical evaluation
