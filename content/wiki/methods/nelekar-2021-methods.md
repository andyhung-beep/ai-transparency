# Nelekar et al. 2021 — Methods

**Summary**: A between-subjects study (N = 61) testing three patterns of tailored BDI-based explanations (belief-based, goal-based, belief-and-goal-based) delivered by an embodied conversational agent (ARU) for managing study stress among Indian university students during COVID-19, measuring stress reduction, trust, and behaviour-change intention.

**Sources**: Nelekar et al._Effectiveness embodied conveersational agents for managing academic stress.pdf

**Research questions addressed**: RQ1, RQ2, RQ3

**Last updated**: 2026-04-19

---

## Study design
experimental (between-subjects)

## Sample
- N: 61
- Population: undergraduate students at an Indian university; self-reported experience of study-related stress during the COVID-19 pandemic
- Domain: higher education / mental health / conversational AI
- Country / region: India
- Recruitment method: convenience sampling from an Indian university (specific recruitment method not further reported)

## Transparency operationalization
- Type: process transparency / natural language explanation — BDI agent discloses reasoning basis for coping behaviour recommendations
- Manipulation or measure: three between-subjects conditions varying the content of the agent's explanations: (1) belief-based — explanation references the student's stated barriers to a target behaviour; (2) goal-based — explanation references the student's stated motivations; (3) belief-and-goal-based — explanation combines both barriers and motivations; agent (ARU) was adapted from a Western BDI agent (Sarah) for Indian cultural context; explanations delivered in natural language through an embodied conversational interface; four target behaviours covered: physical activity, healthy diet, studying in groups, meeting new people
- Scale / instrument name: not applicable (experimental manipulation)
- Number of items: not applicable

## Trust operationalization
- Type: cognitive (ability, integrity) and affective (benevolence, bond)
- Measure: trust measured via adapted Mayer and Davis (1999) scale covering four components: ability, benevolence, integrity, and overall trust (1–5 scale); working alliance measured via Working Alliance Inventory Short Revised (WAI-SR) covering task, goal, and bond dimensions; propensity to trust measured as a dispositional baseline covariate
- Scale / instrument name: Mayer and Davis (1999) trust scale (adapted); Working Alliance Inventory Short Revised (WAI-SR)
- Number of items: not reported (specific item count for adapted Mayer & Davis scale not stated in available sources)
- Behavioural vs self-report: self-report for all trust and working alliance measures; behaviour-change intention assessed via self-report (intention to engage in four target behaviours); actual behaviour change not measured

## Moderators and covariates tested
- Explanation type (belief-based, goal-based, belief-and-goal-based): primary independent variable; no significant between-group differences in trust or working alliance were found — relevant to RQ2
- Personality traits (Big Five): measured; conscientiousness moderated intention to exercise in the belief group; emotional stability and extroversion moderated healthy eating intention in the goal group — relevant to RQ3
- Student achievement aim (GPA aspiration): measured covariate; students aiming for high GPA (8.5–10) showed the only consistent significant behaviour-change intentions — strongest moderator found — relevant to RQ3
- Propensity to trust (dispositional trust): measured as baseline; Indian students showed significantly higher propensity (M > 4.5/5) than Western comparison samples; correlated with trust in ARU — relevant to RQ3 cross-cultural context
- Gender: measured; moderated some behaviour-change intention outcomes

## Statistical approach
- Primary analysis method: Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pre-post stress reduction within each group; between-group comparisons on trust and working alliance scores (specific test not reported); correlation analysis for propensity-to-trust and personality trait relationships
- Software: not reported
- Key model fit or effect size reported: all three groups showed significant stress reduction (p < 0.01, Wilcoxon signed-rank); no significant between-group differences in trust; belief-and-goal group had highest average trust and working alliance scores (means not significantly different); Indian propensity to trust M > 4.5/5 vs. Western comparison M = 3.12/5

## Author-noted limitations
- Small sample (N = 61) limits statistical power for detecting between-group differences in trust
- Single university and student population; cross-cultural generalisability limited
- High dispositional trust in Indian sample may have masked differential effects of explanation type on trust
- Behaviour-change outcomes assessed via intention only; actual behaviour not tracked
- Cultural adaptation of Western BDI agent (Sarah → ARU) was iterative but not formally validated for cultural equivalence
- No long-term follow-up conducted; stress reduction and trust effects may not persist beyond the study session
